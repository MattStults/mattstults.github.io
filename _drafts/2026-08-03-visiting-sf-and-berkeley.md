I'm on a BlueDot Impact career transition grant to move into AI safety. As part of that I spent a week visiting San Francisco and Berkeley. I visited BlueDot Impact, Mox, FAR, and Constellation. Being there made it immediately clear to me why being in person was valuable and why many AI safety organizations seem to prefer in-person work. More importantly, it clarified my vision for what my contribution could look like.

*TL;DR for people transitioning into AI safety:* If you have an avenue for access--e.g. connections with BlueDot Impact, friends at the AI safety spaces--it's hard to imagine the in-person trip not paying off. I now have direct contacts to bounce ideas off of, actual opportunities to help with real problems and a perspective I couldn't have gotten as efficiently in any other way.[^0] The value of the trip is surprisingly real even if, like me, your future is not in the proximity of BART. It's likely valuable regardless of where you are in your career. I would go back for another week in the future and would learn from it--even at the cost of guilt trips from my 6 year old.

## The relevant part of my background

I'm a principal software engineer with about 20 years of experience across Ads, game engines, and production deep-learning ranking systems. A little less than half of my career was at Google. Over this period I've learned that the variable that most impacts my day-to-day happiness and the quality of my output is the team I work with. My teams have been excellent. The cryptography PhD with a background in non-Euclidean geometries who happens to build stats reporting features. The rendering algorithms expert who is also deep into Hilbert space and the Hainish space of Ursula Le Guin. I am transitioning to a career in AI safety from a career I've loved and teams of friends because I want to ensure AI rollout goes well. My impression has been that AI safety is talent constrained and this is something I can contribute. Probably the right move with even a marginal EV to impact existential concerns, but personally hard.

Visiting SF and Berkeley made me reconsider what I thought I was giving up.

## The SF/Berkeley AI safety community

I had a lot of conversations about AI safety on my trip that changed my perspective in ways that would be hard to replicate in 30-minute video conferences or LLM deep dives. But the thing that struck me was the tangential conversations I fell into with complete strangers in these spaces. Example topics so idiosyncratic I'd assigned a near-zero prior on discussing them out loud: The implications of anti-realist morality on whether one should eat lunch on the bus. The visual beauty of Morse code representations. Parallels between 1950s American science fiction and the current state of AI.

These are weird and diverse topics, but the density of fascinating people in the AI safety space made it possible for them to come up organically. My working hypothesis is that the cultural gradients you follow to end up eating lunch at FAR happen to route through a bunch of otherwise unrelated passions, lowering the perplexity of oddball conversations. Example: Probably a higher percentage of AI safety people have read *Player Piano* (Vonnegut's novel on automation replacing human labor) than the percentage of people who have in the Ads space. Or at least that's how it works in my simplistic world model where typical Ads people's light reading is Vickrey's[^1] second-price auctions while typical AI safety people assume second-price auctions have something to do with the sale of abandoned storage units.

The communication efficiency applies to AI safety-specific conversations as well. It's much easier to pivot to a question about the mechanics of [Subliminal Learning](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.14805) when you and the people you're talking to are also coming from a shared initialization, and potentially for a lot of the same reasons.

Many people in SF/Berkeley took time out of their day to explain what they were working on and how it related to real impact. I learned about the very real and practical workflow struggles the community is having interpreting AI Character specs, and got to see the work being done to solve them. Someone took the time to explain [Evaluation Conditioned Training](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/KjZPBes8MsoBFTHR3/teaching-models-to-dream-of-better-monitors-through-monitor) to me and work through whether my concerns were worth looking into. Even the early career people I met were able to explain the connection between their work and its impact in a way that felt unusual, at least in my experience of early career engineers outside of AI safety.

## The Limits of Belonging

And then I went home.

After visiting, I am confident that the best collaboration with the people I met is going to happen in person. I strongly suspect people trying to engage in *this part* of the AI safety community remotely are missing out. Unfortunately, my life obligations make full in-person engagement in the SF/Berkeley community unrealistic. I've got three kids I cannot relocate and an existing community beyond my work. I'm coming from a career averaging 55 to 60 hours a week, and that may still be on the low end of what some people described feeling compelled to contribute. My family needs me to reduce that number as time goes forward, not increase it.

The trip wasn't a disappointment. It was a great trip and gave me every reason to think that the parts of AI safety that *are* compatible with my constraints--potentially FAR specifically--are going to be even more aligned with what I'm looking for. More consequentially, the trip helped me see what I *should not* aim for--not because it isn't awesome but because it's transparently not fit for my situation.

Prior to going to SF/Berkeley I didn't have a concrete enough vision for what AI safety work looked like to appreciate the conflict. Now that I can see the conflict, it has forced me to rethink how I approach contributing--e.g. looking for remote-friendly or local organizations rather than imagining a remote role at an in-person-first org. There are compelling remote-first roles, but not that many--or if there are, please send them to me. My colleagues are actively contacting me about media implying they should contribute to AI safety ([ex](https://www.kucoin.com/news/flash/ai-safety-firm-metr-offers-503k-salaries-but-struggles-to-hire-enough-staff), [ex](https://betakit.com/u-of-t-professor-jacob-tsimerman-who-won-maths-highest-prize-to-join-openai/)). I'm less sure what to say now. I suspect the field is indeed bottlenecked by talent but I don't know how much remote, senior product SWE AI safety can absorb. If you have a clear view on this I'd like to hear it.

## Changes in approach

### What I've been doing so far

I've been doing the career transition grant for about 2 months. My approach has been to try to upskill in AI safety research and make my value legible by (1) applying to AI research fellowships and (2) doing AI safety empirical research--reproducing existing experiments and conducting my own. My high level takeaways from the trip were: (i) This community is potentially a great fit for me, not just the mission I've chosen. (ii) Conversations with real people identified that my AI safety insights are not quite as sharp and load-bearing as Claude said[^2]. (iii) I probably don't need to develop a full mastery over AI safety/empirical research before my existing experience could aid AI safety.

**Interpretation:**
A fellowship would provide legibility and mentorship from the people I'd most like to learn from--I would absolutely take a fellowship opportunity if I could and I will continue to apply as I am able--but I hope my ~20 years of experience and capability assessments provide an equivalent legibility signal for engineering capability today. My engineering experience is empirically valuable: the frontier is excellent but I am still constantly correcting and shaping my own work in measurably[^3] critical ways.

It's hard to admit my failure to be selected for these fellowships[^4]. Part of this is because I have been unsympathetically spoiled in the hiring process as a senior SWE. Anthropic Fellows' nearly 8 hour test battery concluded with a 15-minute interview and a rejection email. It's hard to go from being competed for to competing and losing. Fair, good for safety to have the competition, but personally challenging in a way I need to consciously correct for. The seductive but objectively unearned conclusion: "This space is already well served, maybe oversubscribed. The funnel to value was too hard to find or fit with too low an EV to keep torturing myself with high cost applications and low chance payoffs." I don't have enough data to rule it out, but there's plenty of counter evidence and I'm not yet doing the right things to test it.

And on days I don't receive a rejection email, I'm actually quite confident in the unusual value I bring, right now, to a wide range of tasks. It may even be that a fellowship is neither a necessary nor the most impactful next step for me. While in SF/Berkeley it felt pretty clear to me that I am not a month or two away from being a senior researcher, which is my impression of what the field needs vs the kind of junior engineer with pretensions to senior impact I'd struggle not to be. At this point in my career if I'm going to contribute I want to make senior-shaped contributions. But my meetings in SF/Berkeley reinforced that engineering experience is also needed. I suspect I could contribute meaningfully today as a research engineer, or even just extending my previous role as a C++ optimization engineer in a safety organization.

### Proposed Changes In Approach I Wouldn't Have Made Yet

Overarching principles pulled directly from my trip:

- **Prioritize Full Time Engineering Roles Now** I'll continue to build up my research skills, but I'm going to start applying for engineering roles now.
- **Within impactful AI safety, select for team culture fit:** I want to have a positive impact on safety. I'm excited about research, but the biggest impact I am likely to have in the short/medium term is likely to come from supporting an AI safety organization with an existing agenda using my SWE and general development experience. My experience tells me that the biggest variable to control for my impact--and certainly my happiness--is team fit. This includes being remote-friendly or local.

To date I've been following advice to focus on a specific problem: the emergence of grader awareness. I'm going to broaden my focus without dropping my existing work. I don't have a whole career ahead of me to build expertise around a specific problem I'm worried about but don't yet have the experience to know if it's the right one or actually tractable. I already have expertise from a career. The next steps need to be to figure out the shortest path to using it. And if that doesn't work, grader awareness emergence will (probably/unfortunately) still have open problems.

Next steps:

1. Deeply understand the theories of change and specific impact I could have at remote-friendly and Seattle-area-adjacent offices with AI safety-specific roles (e.g. FAR, Epoch, Google Kirkland, Microsoft).
2. Re-evaluate my world model of AI safety geography in a context where open-weight Kimi K3 and Qwen-3.8 Max arrive on the heels of their API model competitors. Example: is access to the labs necessary in a world where the (near) frontier is increasingly open?
3. Continue to apply to fellowships (super exciting mentor opportunities in SPAR) and work on my own research, but as a secondary path to answering the question: "How can I be helpful today?"
